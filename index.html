Context

Why This Region, Why Now

Mongolia's pastoral livestock sector is the backbone of the rural economy — and one of its most vulnerable. It is also the living core of Mongolian identity: a nomadic heritage built on an inseparable bond between herder, herd and pasture. Decades of environmental, agricultural stewardship and diplomacy have laid the groundwork for a sustainable sector. Delivering it now demands participation at every level.

That's 77% above the scientifically calculated sustainable capacity (0.427 SU/ha, national forage-balance method). After losing over half their livestock in the 2023 dzud, herders are rebuilding herds as their main income strategy — deepening pressure on land.

Year 3 target: 135,000 SU → reduces the gap from 77% to ~41% above capacity · contributing 0.2% of the national GHG goal by 2035.

Policy & Technology on Pasture Utilization and Monitoring

Built On National Policy, Delivered at Herder Level

The Government of Mongolia, in cooperation with international projects and programs (GEF, UN FAO, UNDP, WWF, GCF, TNC, Switch-Asia etc), has systematically implemented state policy initiatives aimed at pastureland conservation. These efforts include developing methodologies for calculating carrying capacity on special pastures, expanding protected areas, and conducting photo monitoring of rangelands. Consequently, suitable methodologies, technologies, and governance frameworks for pastureland conservation have been successfully launched.

Moving forward, maintaining pasture sustainability by leveraging these established models, methodologies, and technologies is a responsibility that must begin at the level of individual herders, herder organizations, and baghs. Within the Tavlag Bayangol Project, the objective is to educate and familiarize herders with best practices already piloted — developing pasture usage plans and rotational resting of rangelands with local land management authorities, to enhance livestock value while preserving nomadic heritage and biodiversity.

Why a Herder-Owned Model

Those Who Bear the Risk Hold the Responsibility

Herders' lives are tied to the pasture — conscious of holding primary responsibility for how their land is managed. Herders are the first to feel a bad dzud, the first to lose income when the land degrades. That direct exposure is exactly why herders lead, with outside operators enabling rather than driving the action.

Live on the Pasture

Most exposed to drought, dzud, and degradation — not an abstract risk, a daily one.

Highest Stake, Highest Responsibility

Investors and operators are important to enable the action. Herders carry the risk day to day.

Organized & Invested, Themselves

34 households chose direct ownership — not membership on paper, capital in the ground.

That's why the herders themselves initiated this cooperative. Support of UN FAO and WWF through the "Dornod-Kheer" project, funded by GEF-7, is valuable on the land.

Pillars 3–5 in Practice — The Livestock Layer

Better Genetics, Healthier Animals, Feed That Depressurizes Pasture

Three pillars, $195k in grants · the livestock layer that turns pasture recovery into higher-value animals.

Pillar 3 — Breeding

Problem: quantity has been prioritised over quality, stalling genetic gain.
Action: ratio-based rams and bucks, one per 50 breeding females, phased in.
$72k grant

Pillar 4 — Veterinary

Problem: five disease threats, foot-and-mouth among them.
Action: GAP training — sanitation, dipping, deworming — plus the Pillar 2 water fix.

Pillar 5 — Organic Feed

Problem: lost plant diversity leaves animals mineral-deficient in winter.
Action: mineral and concentrate supplement for 10% of the confirmed herd (6,863 head).
